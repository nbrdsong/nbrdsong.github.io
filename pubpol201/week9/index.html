Debrief

What the game hid

Each policy sets a target level for each metric. The true underlying level does not jump instantly to that target; it adjusts gradually toward it each year at a fixed rate. Each year also adds a hidden random shock—a one-year bump or dip caused by something outside the policy, such as a new curriculum, a flu outbreak, or a change in the test. The noise is autocorrelated: a shock in one year tends to carry into the next, so observed changes can persist even when the true level is moving toward its target. You only saw the observed number, which mixes the adjustment path and the noise. So a rise or fall in the observed number does not by itself prove the policy caused it.

For example, if reading starts at 28% and a policy sets a target of 40%, the true level moves gradually toward 40%—not in a straight line—and observed scores bounce around that path because of noise. A 3-point observed rise could be partly adjustment and partly shock. Real researchers use comparison groups, multiple years, and quasi-experimental designs to separate policy effects from other factors.

This is the part policymakers cannot see: the model’s target level, the gradual adjustment path (latent), and the hidden shock. The observed number is what legislators see. Observed change is the adjustment toward the target plus noise, not a clean estimate of the policy effect.

What the readings suggest

ReformEvidence
School finance Additional spending generally improves test scores and educational attainment, especially for low-income students. Effects are often modest and depend on how money is allocated and implemented.
School accountability Test-based accountability is associated with math gains, especially for disadvantaged students. Concerns include narrowed curriculum, teaching to the test, unrealistic proficiency targets, and pressure without support.
School choice Charters on average perform similarly to traditional public schools, with stronger positive effects for urban low-income students. Vouchers and ESAs can produce negative achievement effects and increase segregation; accountability and oversight matter.
