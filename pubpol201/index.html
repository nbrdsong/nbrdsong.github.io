Central Bank of Weillland

Board of Governors

You set the rules for the housing and banking system of a $14 trillion economy.

Learning objectives
  • Judging whether a market is failing, and in what way
  • Deciding when government should intervene, and weighing what intervention costs against what it prevents
  • Making policy without knowing how it will turn out, and living with the result

Who is on the Board?

Choose a Chair

Every policy is decided by a vote of the Board. If a vote is tied, the Chair decides.

Where things stand

The economy is strong. Home prices have risen every quarter for a decade. Record home ownership, booming construction, low unemployment.

Despite this, a few economists have raised concerns about potential market failures.

You have to choose whether to intervene, and if so, how.

The Bank's mandate is 2% real growth per year. You will be judged against it.

Each quarter the Board votes on four policies. Changing a policy imposes an economic cost of $50B per policy changed. Then the quarter plays out probabilistically, on a d20.

      What people will say

      Beyond the number

      The mandate measured growth. It did not measure who received it, who carried the losses when things broke, or whether the rules you set were fair to the people they applied to.

      A utilitarian adds it all up and asks whether the total went up. A Rawlsian asks how the worst-off did, and whether they would have agreed to these rules without knowing they would be the worst-off. A Nozickian asks whether anyone's rights were violated along the way, and would count your regulations among the violations.

      Some of those positions say a slower economy can be the right answer. Some say the crash is not the worst thing that can happen. The number above is one input to that argument, not the argument.
